<p>The first time I made a pitcher sangria spritz, I was looking for something that felt special but didn't require a bartending degree. You know that moment when you want a drink that's festive, fruity, and still light enough to sip on a warm afternoon? This is it. The key is letting the fruit sit in the wine long enough to release its juices, then adding the sparkle at the very end. The result is a drink that's more than the sum of its parts—each sip carries a hint of orange, a whisper of berry, and a clean, bubbly finish.</p>

<h2>The Ratio That Works Every Time</h2>
<p>The most common mistake I see with sangria spritz is drowning the wine in soda. You end up with a watery, sad drink. I keep the ratio at three parts wine to one part sparkling water. For a standard 750 ml bottle of wine, that means about 2 cups of sparkling water. This gives you a light, effervescent drink where the wine still shines. If you prefer a fizzier spritz, go to a 2:1 ratio, but don't go beyond that, or the fruit flavors get lost.</p>

<h2>Choose the Right Wine Base</h2>
<p>I use a dry red wine like Tempranillo or Grenache. They're not too tannic, and they have enough fruit character to complement the fresh fruit you'll add. Avoid anything too oaky or heavy, like a bold Cabernet Sauvignon—it will clash with the lightness of the spritz. For a white version, a dry Sauvignon Blanc or Pinot Grigio works beautifully, with white peach, green apple, and lemon slices.</p>

<h2>The Fruit Matters</h2>
<p>I use a mix of citrus and berries. Oranges bring a bright, aromatic note. Apples add a crisp texture that holds up well without getting mushy. Strawberries and grapes soften slightly as they soak, releasing their sweetness into the wine. Slice everything thinly—about 1/4 inch thick—so the flavors infuse quickly. If you cut them too thick, they won't release enough juice in the time you have.</p>

<h2>Let It Rest (But Not Too Long)</h2>
<p>After you combine the wine, liquor, simple syrup, and fruit, cover the pitcher and refrigerate it for at least 2 hours. This is where the magic happens. The fruit softens and the wine takes on those fresh flavors. I've let it go up to 8 hours, and it only gets better. But don't let it sit overnight—the fruit can start to break down and make the sangria cloudy and slightly bitter, especially the citrus pith.</p>

<h3>A Note on the Simple Syrup</h3>
<p>I make a quick simple syrup by combining equal parts sugar and water in a small saucepan, heating until the sugar dissolves, then cooling it. It's easier to blend than granulated sugar, which can sink to the bottom of the pitcher. Start with 1/4 cup of syrup, taste, and add more if you like it sweeter. The fruit will also add sweetness, so you may need less than you think.</p>

<h2>Add the Fizz at the End</h2>
<p>This is non-negotiable. Pour the sparkling water in just before you serve, and stir once or twice very gently. If you stir too hard, you'll lose the carbonation. I use chilled sparkling water straight from the fridge to keep everything cold without needing tons of ice, which would dilute the drink. Speaking of ice, fill each glass with ice cubes, then pour the sangria spritz over it. This keeps the pitcher from getting watered down.</p>

<h2>The Little Details That Make a Difference</h2>
<p>I always include a few pieces of fruit in each glass. It looks beautiful and gives guests something to nibble on as they sip. A sprig of fresh mint adds a clean, herbal note that lifts the whole drink. If you're serving a crowd, set out a small bowl of extra fruit and mint so people can customize their glass.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<p>One: using a sweet wine. Stick with dry wine and control sweetness with the simple syrup. Two: adding soda too early. The bubbles will be gone before you pour. Three: skipping the rest time. The fruit needs at least a couple of hours to infuse. Four: using overripe fruit—it can turn mushy and make the drink murky. Choose fruit that's ripe but still firm.</p>

<h2>Variations to Try</h2>
<p>For a white sangria spritz, swap the red wine for a dry Sauvignon Blanc. Use sliced white peaches, green apple, and lemon. Replace the brandy with elderflower liqueur or vodka. For a rosé version, use a dry rosé and add raspberries, sliced peaches, and a splash of St-Germain. The method stays the same: let it rest, then add the fizz.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>A pitcher sangria spritz is one of the easiest ways to serve a crowd without standing at the bar all night. The key is the ratio, the rest time, and adding the fizz at the last minute. Once you've got that down, you can play with different fruits and wines to make it your own. The whole point is to enjoy the process and the company—and this drink makes both easy.</p>
