<p>There's a moment when you cut into a perfectly ripe tomato in July—the knife meets just a little resistance, then glides through. The flesh is firm but giving, the skin taut, the scent sweet and earthy. That's the tomato I wait for all year. And when I get it, I make pico de gallo.</p><p>Not a watery, sad version that pools on the plate after ten minutes. I mean a pico de gallo where each chunk of tomato holds its shape, the onion has a clean bite, and the lime and cilantro taste like they just met. The trick is in the technique, not the ingredients. You likely already have everything you need.</p><h2>Choose the Right Tomato</h2><p>Roma tomatoes are my go-to for pico de gallo. They have fewer seeds, thicker walls, and less jelly than round slicing tomatoes. That means less liquid to deal with from the start. Look for tomatoes that feel heavy for their size and yield slightly to gentle pressure—not mushy, not rock hard. If they smell like a tomato at the stem end, you're in good shape.</p><p>If you can only find beefsteak or vine-ripened tomatoes, they work too. Just know you'll need to be extra thorough when removing the seeds and jelly. Cherry tomatoes are too sweet and delicate for a classic pico, in my opinion. Save those for a salad.</p><h2>The Ratio That Works</h2><p>I keep the ratio at roughly 4:1:1:1 by volume—tomatoes to onion to jalapeño to cilantro. That's four parts diced tomato, one part chopped white onion, one part chopped jalapeño, and one part chopped cilantro. The lime juice and salt are measured separately. This ratio gives you a balanced pico where no single flavor dominates. You taste the tomato first, then the heat and herb follow.</p><p>For a standard batch, that means 4 medium Roma tomatoes (about 1 1/2 pounds), 1/2 cup finely chopped white onion, 2 tablespoons seeded and chopped jalapeño, and 1/2 cup packed cilantro leaves, chopped. Two tablespoons of lime juice and about 1/2 teaspoon of kosher salt finish it.</p><h2>Salt the Tomatoes First</h2><p>This is the step most recipes skip, and it's the one that makes the difference. After you dice the tomatoes (after coring and removing the seeds and jelly), toss them with 1/2 teaspoon of kosher salt. Let them sit for exactly 5 minutes. The salt draws out the water trapped inside the tomato cells. You'll see a puddle of pinkish liquid form at the bottom of the bowl.</p><p>Pour the tomatoes into a colander and let that liquid drain off. Do not press or squeeze them—just let gravity do its work for about 30 seconds. You lose a little volume, but you gain a pico that stays chunky for hours instead of turning into soup. The salt stays on the tomatoes, so you don't lose seasoning.</p><h3>Rinse the Onion</h3><p>Chopped raw white onion is pungent—that's part of the appeal. But if you add it straight to the pico, it can overwhelm everything else. A quick rinse under cold water in a fine-mesh strainer washes away some of the sulfurous compounds that make onion harsh. Shake off the excess water and add it to the bowl. The onion stays crunchy but loses that aggressive bite.</p><h2>Let It Rest, Briefly</h2><p>Once everything is combined, let the pico de gallo sit at room temperature for 10 minutes before serving. This short rest lets the lime juice and salt work into the vegetables without breaking down the tomato structure. If you refrigerate it right away, the cold dulls the flavors. If you let it sit too long (over an hour), the tomatoes start to soften. Ten minutes is the sweet spot.</p><p>Taste it after the rest. Adjust salt or lime if needed. Sometimes a tomato is sweeter than expected and needs an extra squeeze. Sometimes the jalapeño is mild and you want a pinch more. Trust your palate.</p><h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2><ul><li><strong>Skipping the seed removal:</strong> The jelly around the seeds is mostly water. Leave it in, and your pico will weep within minutes.</li><li><strong>Using a food processor:</strong> A few pulses turn tomatoes into mush. Hand-dice everything for a clean, chunky texture.</li><li><strong>Adding the lime juice too early:</strong> Lime juice is acidic and starts to cook the tomatoes. Add it just before serving, or at most 15 minutes ahead.</li><li><strong>Over-salting:</strong> You can always add more salt, but you can't take it out. Start with 1/2 teaspoon for a batch this size.</li></ul><p>Pico de gallo is one of those rare recipes where the technique matters more than the ingredient list. Nail the moisture control and the ratio, and you'll have a salsa that tastes like summer in a bowl—bright, crunchy, and alive. Serve it with tortilla chips, spoon it over tacos, or eat it straight from the bowl with a fork. I won't judge.</p>
