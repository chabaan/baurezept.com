<p>The scent of lemon and garlic hitting hot grill grates is one of those smells that says summer is here. But getting chicken to stay juicy instead of turning into a dry, flavorless puck? That takes a little know-how. My approach relies on a simple marinade with a specific acid-to-oil ratio and careful temperature control. No mushy meat, no burnt edges—just bright, herbaceous chicken with a golden char.</p>
<h2>Why This Ratio Matters</h2>
<p>Most marinades are either too acidic or too oily. I keep the ratio at 3 parts oil to 1 part acid. That's 1/4 cup olive oil to 3 tablespoons lemon juice. The oil carries the fat-soluble flavors from the herbs and garlic deep into the meat. The acid tenderizes just enough without turning the surface into mush. If you go overboard on lemon juice, the chicken's outer layer can become mealy after a few hours. Stick to that 3:1 ratio, and you'll get a balanced marinade that works every time.</p>
<h2>The Right Herbs for the Job</h2>
<p>Dried oregano and thyme are my go-tos here. They hold up to the heat of the grill without burning, unlike fresh herbs which can blacken and turn bitter. Dried herbs also infuse more flavor during the marinating time because they rehydrate in the oil and lemon juice. If you prefer fresh, triple the amount (1 tablespoon fresh oregano instead of 1 teaspoon dried) and add them in the last hour of marinating so they don't wilt completely.</p>
<h2>Temperature Is Everything</h2>
<p>Grilling chicken isn't about guessing. I use an instant-read thermometer every time. For boneless breasts, medium-high heat (400-450°F) gives you a good sear without burning the outside before the inside cooks through. Cook 5-6 minutes per side without moving the chicken. If you try to flip it too early, it will stick. When it releases easily, it's ready to turn.</p>
<p>For the juiciest results, pull the chicken at 160°F (71°C). Carryover cooking will raise the temperature to the safe 165°F (74°C) during the 5-minute rest. Skip the rest, and you'll lose those juices onto the cutting board instead of keeping them in the meat.</p>
<h2>Common Mistake to Avoid</h2>
<p>Don't marinate longer than 4 hours. I know it's tempting to set it up in the morning and grill at dinner, but the acid will break down the protein structure too much. The chicken turns soft and almost pasty. Thirty minutes is enough for good flavor; 4 hours is the absolute max. Set a timer if you need to.</p>
<h2>Serving and Variations</h2>
<p>Let the chicken rest on a cutting board for 5 minutes before slicing. Serve with fresh lemon slices—they add a bright pop of acidity that wakes up the whole dish. This chicken works well alongside grilled vegetables, a simple rice pilaf, or a crisp Greek salad. For a different twist, swap the dried thyme for rosemary or add a pinch of red pepper flakes for heat.</p>
<h3>Handling Leftovers Safely</h3>
<p>Refrigerate any leftovers within 2 hours. They'll keep for up to 3 days in an airtight container. Reheat gently in a skillet over medium-low heat with a splash of water or broth to bring back moisture. You can also slice the cold chicken over salads for lunch the next day.</p>
<h2>A Final Takeaway</h2>
<p>Great grilled lemon herb chicken comes down to three things: a balanced marinade, precise heat, and knowing when to pull it off the grill. Master those, and you'll have a weeknight dinner that feels like a summer celebration—without the stress.</p>
