<p>There's a moment in late summer when peaches are so fragrant you can smell them from across the kitchen. That's when I swap tomatoes for peaches in a classic caprese. The sweetness of the fruit, the creaminess of fresh mozzarella, and the peppery bite of basil — it's a combination that works because of contrast. And the balsamic glaze? It ties everything together with a tangy-sweet ribbon.</p>

<h2>Why Peaches Work in Caprese</h2>
<p>A good caprese relies on the balance of acid, fat, and salt. Tomatoes bring acidity; mozzarella brings fat; basil adds freshness. Peaches, when ripe, have a natural acidity that mimics tomatoes but with a floral sweetness. The key is choosing fruit that's ripe but still firm. Too soft, and the slices will collapse. Too hard, and they'll taste sour and astringent.</p>

<p>I keep the ratio simple: one peach, one 4-ounce ball of fresh mozzarella, and a handful of basil leaves for every two servings. That gives you enough for a generous side salad or a light lunch with crusty bread.</p>

<h2>The Technique: Balsamic Glaze from Scratch</h2>
<p>Store-bought balsamic glaze is convenient, but making it yourself takes five minutes and gives you control over sweetness and thickness. The trick is to use a good balsamic vinegar — nothing aged for decades, just a decent supermarket brand. I add a spoonful of honey or brown sugar to balance the vinegar's sharpness.</p>

<p>Here's the method: In a small saucepan, combine 1/4 cup balsamic vinegar and 1 tablespoon honey. Bring it to a gentle simmer over medium heat — you'll see small bubbles around the edge. Then reduce the heat to low and let it cook, stirring occasionally, until it coats the back of a spoon. That usually takes about 5 to 6 minutes. Watch closely toward the end; it can go from syrupy to burnt in seconds.</p>

<p>Once it's done, pour it into a small bowl to cool. It will continue to thicken as it sits. If it gets too thick, stir in a few drops of water or warm it for a few seconds in the microwave.</p>

<h2>Assembling the Salad</h2>
<p>Slice the peaches into even wedges — about 1/4-inch thick. Slice the mozzarella into similar-sized rounds. If the mozzarella is very soft, use a sharp knife and clean it between cuts to keep the slices neat.</p>

<p>On a large platter, arrange the slices in a single overlapping layer. I start with a peach slice, then mozzarella, then a basil leaf, and repeat. For larger basil leaves, I tear them in half so they don't overwhelm a single bite. The visual is part of the experience — a pattern of pale orange, white, and green.</p>

<p>Drizzle the olive oil first, not the glaze. Oil coats the cheese and fruit, letting the flavors meld. Then season with flaky sea salt and a few cracks of black pepper. Finally, drizzle the balsamic glaze in thin ribbons — use a spoon and move your hand in sweeping lines across the platter.</p>

<h2>Common Mistake: Overdressing</h2>
<p>The biggest mistake I see is drowning the salad in glaze. A little goes a long way. You want each bite to get a hint of sweetness, not a sticky coating. Start with half the glaze, taste a piece of peach and cheese together, then add more if needed.</p>

<p>Another mistake: using cold mozzarella straight from the fridge. Let it sit at room temperature for 15 minutes before slicing. Cold cheese is rubbery and won't release its full creamy flavor.</p>

<h2>Variations to Try</h2>
<p>Once you have the basic method down, you can play with the flavors. Add a handful of arugula for peppery crunch. Swap basil for mint or tarragon — both pair beautifully with peach. For a bit of heat, sprinkle with red pepper flakes. Or add toasted pine nuts or slivered almonds for texture.</p>

<p>If you're serving this as a main course, add grilled chicken or shrimp. The sweet-salty profile works well with smoky char.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>Fresh Peach Caprese Salad is proof that the best summer cooking often requires no heat at all. The quality of the ingredients does the work. Choose ripe peaches, good mozzarella, and fresh basil, and you'll have a dish that's greater than the sum of its parts. Serve it alongside grilled meat, with crusty bread, or simply on its own as a light lunch.</p>
