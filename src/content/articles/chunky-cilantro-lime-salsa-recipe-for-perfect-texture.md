<p>The first time I made salsa at home, it came out watery and flat. The tomatoes released all their juice, the lime was barely there, and the cilantro tasted like an afterthought. That’s when I started paying attention to ratios and technique. This chunky cilantro-lime salsa is the result: bold, bright, and built for texture.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/c9b65f1a-5eae-4f00-979a-ba5c03d68eb0/0_2.png" alt="Seeding a Roma tomato by squeezing out the seeds over a bowl" /></figure>
<p>The secret is how you handle the tomatoes. Roma tomatoes are my go-to because they have fewer seeds and firmer flesh. Cut each one in half crosswise, then gently squeeze out the seeds and jelly. This one step keeps your salsa from turning into tomato soup. Dice the flesh into ¼-inch pieces — not too small, or you lose the chunky feel.</p><h2>Why This Ratio Works</h2><p>I keep the ratio at roughly 4 parts tomato to 1 part onion, with just enough lime and salt to wake everything up. For every 4 medium Roma tomatoes, use ½ cup finely diced red onion. Soaking the onion in cold water for five minutes removes some of the sharp sulfur compounds, leaving a mild crunch. Drain and pat it dry before adding to the bowl.</p><p>Jalapeño adds heat, but it's controllable. One seeded jalapeño gives a gentle warmth. If you want more kick, leave a few seeds in. Mince it finely so no one gets a surprise chunk of pepper.</p><h2>The Lime-Cilantro Balance</h2><p>Fresh lime juice is non-negotiable. You need about ¼ cup for this batch, which is roughly two juicy limes. Roll the limes on the counter before juicing to release more liquid. The acidity should be present but not puckering — it brightens the tomatoes without overwhelming them.</p><p>Cilantro is the star here. Use only the leaves and tender stems. Chop them finely, but not to a paste. You want little flecks of green throughout. Half a cup packed leaves is the sweet spot. Too much, and it tastes like grass; too little, and the dish falls flat.</p><h3>The One Spice That Makes a Difference</h3><p>Ground cumin might seem out of place, but a quarter teaspoon adds a warm, earthy note that ties everything together. It's not enough to taste cumin specifically — it just makes the salsa taste more complete. Don't skip it.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Serving Too Soon</h2><p>Let the salsa rest at room temperature for at least 10 minutes. This gives the salt time to draw out a little moisture and allows the flavors to marry. If you serve it immediately, the lime and salt sit on top of the tomatoes instead of soaking in. After resting, give it a quick stir. If there's excess liquid pooling at the bottom, spoon it off.</p><h2>How to Use This Salsa</h2><p>This chunky cilantro-lime salsa is perfect with tortilla chips, but it also shines on tacos, grilled chicken, fish, or scrambled eggs. I often spoon it over black beans and rice for a quick bowl. It keeps in the fridge for up to three days, though the cilantro will darken slightly. That's fine — the flavor stays bright.</p><p>One last tip: taste as you go. Salt and lime are personal. Start with the amounts listed, then adjust. You'll know it's right when the salsa tastes fresh, tangy, and a little bit addicting.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>How do I keep my salsa from getting watery?</h3>
<p>The key is seeding the tomatoes. Cut them in half crosswise and squeeze out the seeds and jelly before dicing. Also, let the finished salsa rest for 10 minutes, then drain off any excess liquid that collects before serving.</p>
<h3>Can I use bottled lime juice?</h3>
<p>Fresh lime juice gives a brighter, cleaner flavor. Bottled juice often has a flat, slightly bitter taste. I recommend using fresh limes for this recipe.</p>
<h3>How spicy is this salsa?</h3>
<p>With one seeded jalapeño, it's mild to medium. For a milder version, use only half the jalapeño. For more heat, leave in some seeds or add a second jalapeño.</p>
