<p>The first bite of a good lemon curd tart hits you in two waves: the crisp, buttery shell shatters, then the filling floods your mouth with a brightness that wakes up your whole palate. It's not just sweet—it's sharp, sunny, and clean. And it's one of those desserts that looks impressive but is built on a few simple techniques you can master in one afternoon.</p>

<h2>Why This Lemon Curd Tart Works</h2>
<p>The secret to a great lemon curd tart is balance. The shell needs to be sturdy enough to hold the filling without getting soggy, and the curd needs to be thick enough to slice but still silky on the tongue. My approach uses a classic pâte sucrée (a sweet shortcrust) that's enriched with an egg yolk for extra tenderness, and a lemon curd that's cooked slowly over gentle heat to avoid scrambling the eggs. The ratio that never fails me: 1 part lemon juice to 1 part sugar by volume, with 3 eggs per half-cup of juice. That gives you a curd that sets firmly but stays luscious.</p>

<h2>The Tart Shell: Crisp, Not Crumbly</h2>
<p>Start with cold butter. Cut it into the flour mixture until you see pea-sized butter lumps—those create the flaky layers. Adding the egg yolk gives the dough a richer flavor and a more tender crumb than a standard pie crust. After mixing, chill the dough for at least 30 minutes. This relaxes the gluten and firms the butter, which prevents shrinking during baking.</p>
<p>When you roll it out, work on a lightly floured surface and turn the dough frequently to keep it from sticking. Aim for an even 1/8-inch thickness. Press the dough gently into your tartlet pans—don't stretch it, or it will pull back in the oven. Trim the edges flush with the rim using your thumb or a sharp knife.</p>
<p>Blind baking is non-negotiable here. Line each shell with parchment and fill with pie weights or dried beans. Bake at 375°F (190°C) for 12 minutes, then remove the weights and bake another 5-7 minutes. The shells should look pale golden and feel dry to the touch. If they still look wet in the center, give them another 2 minutes. Let them cool completely before adding the curd—warm shells will make the curd soak in and turn soggy.</p>

<h2>The Lemon Curd: Silky, Bright, and Foolproof</h2>
<p>Whisk the eggs and sugar together first, until the sugar dissolves and the mixture looks pale. Then add the lemon juice and zest. Zest is crucial—it carries the essential oils that give the curd its aromatic punch. Use a microplane or fine grater, and only take the yellow part, not the white pith underneath, which is bitter.</p>
<p>Cook the mixture over medium-low heat, whisking constantly. This is the part where patience pays off. The curd will go from thin and foamy to thick and glossy in about 6-8 minutes. The visual cue: it should coat the back of a spoon, and when you run your finger through it, the line holds. The temperature cue: 170°F (77°C) on an instant-read thermometer. If it hits 180°F (82°C), you're on the edge of scrambled eggs, so pull it off the heat immediately.</p>
<p>Strain the curd through a fine-mesh sieve into a clean bowl. This catches any accidental cooked bits and guarantees a smooth texture. Let it cool for just a minute or two, then whisk in the butter, piece by piece. The butter adds richness and a velvety mouthfeel. Once it's all in, the curd will look glossy and thick.</p>

<h2>Assembling the Tarts</h2>
<p>Pour the warm curd into the cooled shells, filling them almost to the rim. Don't overfill, or the curd will spill over as it sets. Let them cool to room temperature on the counter (about 20 minutes), then transfer to the refrigerator. Chill for at least 2 hours. The curd will firm up into a sliceable consistency that still feels soft on the tongue.</p>
<p>I like to garnish with a dusting of powdered sugar and a single fresh blueberry or raspberry on top—the tartness of the berry echoes the lemon, and the sugar adds a pretty finish. But they're just as lovely on their own.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Boiling the curd.</strong> If you see bubbles, you've gone too far. The eggs will scramble, and you'll get a grainy texture. Keep the heat low and whisk constantly.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping the strain.</strong> Even if you think you whisked perfectly, strain the curd. It's cheap insurance against lumps.</li>
<li><strong>Filling warm shells.</strong> The shells must be completely cool before you add the curd. Warmth draws moisture into the crust, making it soft.</li>
<li><strong>Using cold butter in the curd.</strong> Room-temperature butter emulsifies into the warm curd smoothly. Cold butter will seize up and leave greasy spots.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>Once you've made lemon curd from scratch, you'll never go back to the store-bought stuff. It's brighter, cleaner, and you control the sweetness. These tarts are perfect for a spring brunch, a tea party, or just a quiet afternoon treat. The technique is simple, but the result feels special.</p>
