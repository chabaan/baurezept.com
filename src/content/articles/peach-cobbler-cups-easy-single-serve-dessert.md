<p>The smell of peaches bubbling under a buttery biscuit crust is pure summer. These Peach Cobbler Cups concentrate all that comfort into individual ramekins, so everyone gets their own golden-brown topping and spoonfuls of tender fruit. No fighting over the corner piece.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/7df73136-0260-4bbf-97aa-840e58525d4f/0_2.png" alt="Spoon dropping biscuit dough over sliced peaches in a ramekin" /></figure>
<p>The key is a biscuit topping with distinct layers—not a dense dough. I keep the ratio of cold butter to flour at about 1:4 by weight, which creates steam pockets that lift the crust into something flaky and tender. And baking them on a sheet pan catches any drips and makes handling easy.</p><h2>Choosing and Prepping the Peaches</h2><p>For the best texture, use peaches that are ripe but still firm—they should give slightly when pressed but not be mushy. Freestone varieties (like Redhaven or Elberta) are easiest to slice because the flesh separates cleanly from the pit. To peel, score a small X on the bottom of each peach, blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds, then transfer to an ice bath. The skins slip right off.</p><p>If fresh peaches aren’t in season, frozen sliced peaches (thawed and drained) or canned peaches in juice work well. Just adjust the sugar as noted in the FAQ.</p><h2>The Biscuit Topping: Technique Matters</h2><p>Cold butter is non-negotiable here. Cut it into small cubes and return it to the fridge while you prep the peaches. When you cut the butter into the flour mixture, work quickly—you want visible pea-sized butter pieces throughout. Those bits melt in the oven, creating steam that separates the dough into tender layers.</p><p>Add the milk and vanilla all at once, then stir with a fork just until the dough comes together. It will look shaggy and a bit dry. That’s exactly right. Overmixing gives you a tough, hockey-puck biscuit.</p><h3>Why Ramekins?</h3><p>Individual ramekins (6-ounce size is ideal) ensure even baking and a higher topping-to-filling ratio. The sides of the ramekin support the biscuit as it rises, so it puffs up rather than spreading out. If you don’t have ramekins, a standard 8x8-inch baking dish works—just increase the bake time by 5 to 8 minutes.</p><h2>Baking for Golden Perfection</h2><p>Place the ramekins on a baking sheet to catch any overflow. Bake at 375°F (190°C) for 18 to 22 minutes. At the 15-minute mark, check the color of the biscuit tops. If they’re browning too quickly, tent loosely with foil for the remaining time. The filling should be bubbling thickly around the edges—that’s how you know the cornstarch has activated and the peaches are perfectly tender.</p><p>Let the cups rest for 5 minutes after baking. The filling will thicken slightly as it cools, and the biscuit will set. Serve warm, plain or with a scoop of vanilla ice cream.</p><h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2><ul><li><strong>Using warm butter:</strong> Warm butter blends into the flour, creating a dense dough. Keep everything cold until it hits the oven.</li><li><strong>Skipping the lemon juice:</strong> A teaspoon of lemon juice brightens the peach flavor and prevents the fruit from turning brown. Don’t omit it.</li><li><strong>Overfilling the ramekins:</strong> Leave about 1/2 inch of space at the top. The filling bubbles up, and the biscuit needs room to rise.</li></ul><h2>Storage and Reheating</h2><p>Leftover cups keep in the refrigerator for up to 3 days, covered tightly. The biscuit will soften slightly from the moisture of the filling. To restore some crispness, reheat in a 350°F oven for 8 to 10 minutes. Microwaving works in a pinch but will make the topping soggy.</p><p>These cups are also freezer-friendly: bake and cool completely, then wrap each ramekin in plastic wrap and foil. Freeze for up to 2 months. Thaw overnight in the fridge and reheat as above.</p><h2>Takeaway</h2><p>Peach Cobbler Cups are a straightforward dessert that shines because of technique—cold butter, minimal mixing, and the right bake time. Each cup delivers a personal portion of juicy peaches and flaky biscuit, with no leftovers to argue over. Make them once, and you’ll have the method memorized.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I use canned peaches instead of fresh?</h3>
<p>Yes. Drain 1 (15-ounce) can of sliced peaches in juice (not syrup) and reduce the sugar in the filling to 2 tablespoons. Pat the peaches dry before mixing.</p>
<h3>Why did my biscuit topping turn out dense?</h3>
<p>Overmixing the dough develops gluten and makes biscuits tough. Stir just until the dry ingredients are moistened—a few lumps are fine. Also, ensure your butter is very cold.</p>
<h3>How do I know when the cups are done?</h3>
<p>The filling should be bubbling visibly around the edges of the ramekin, and the biscuit topping should be deep golden brown. A toothpick inserted into the biscuit should come out clean.</p>

Stir gently until peaches are coated. Divide peach mixture evenly among ramekins.</li><li>In a separate bowl, whisk together flour, 1/4 cup sugar, baking powder, and 1/4 teaspoon salt. Cut in cold butter using a pastry cutter or two forks until mixture resembles coarse crumbs with pea-sized butter pieces.</li><li>Add milk and vanilla extract to flour mixture. Stir just until a shaggy dough forms—do not overmix. Drop spoonfuls of dough over peaches in each ramekin, covering most of the surface. Sprinkle tops with coarse sugar.</li><li>Bake for 18-22 minutes, until the filling is bubbly around the edges and the biscuit topping is golden brown and cooked through. Let cool for 5 minutes before serving. Serve warm.</li></ol>	</div>
