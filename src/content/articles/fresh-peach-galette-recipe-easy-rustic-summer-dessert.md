<p>The aroma of peaches and cinnamon fills the kitchen as this galette bakes. It's a simpler, more forgiving cousin to a classic pie. No crimping, no perfect edges—just juicy fruit cradled in a flaky, buttery crust. This fresh peach galette is exactly what a summer dessert should be: unfussy, beautiful, and absolutely delicious.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/01f5444e-bf41-4434-8e0a-383cc0e36847/0_3.png" alt="Peach slices being folded into a galette crust on a parchment-lined baking sheet" /></figure>


<h2>Why a Galette Works for Peaches</h2>
<p>A galette is a free-form tart, and that shape is its superpower. You don't need a pie dish or a tart pan. The open center allows the peach filling to caramelize and bubble, concentrating its flavor. The folded edges create a thick, sturdy crust that stays crisp while holding all that juicy goodness. I keep the ratio of fruit to crust generous—about 4 peaches for a 12-inch round of dough. That gives you a satisfying bite of fruit in every slice.</p>

<h2>Key Technique: The Right Peach</h2>
<p>Choose peaches that are ripe but still firm. A peach that yields slightly to gentle pressure is perfect. Overly soft, mushy peaches will turn the filling into a soupy mess. Under-ripe peaches lack sweetness and won't soften enough during the 35-minute bake. If your peaches are a bit tart, increase the sugar by a tablespoon. The lemon juice isn't just for flavor—its acidity helps the cornstarch set the filling properly.</p>

<h2>Building the Flaky Crust</h2>
<p>The crust follows a simple ratio: 1 1/4 cups flour to 1/2 cup butter. The butter must be cold. When it melts in the oven, the steam creates pockets of air, giving you flaky layers. I cut the butter into cubes and work it into the flour until I see pea-sized pieces. Some larger bits are good—they create those desirable shatteringly crisp spots. When you add the ice water, use a fork and stir just until the dough starts to come together. If it feels dry, add another tablespoon of water. But no more than that. A wet dough makes a tough crust.</p>

<h3>Chilling Is Non-Negotiable</h3>
<p>Once the dough is formed into a disk, wrap it and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes. This relaxes the gluten (which prevents shrinkage) and re-chills the butter. If you skip this step, the crust will spread and lose its shape in the oven. You can make the dough a day ahead and keep it in the fridge.</p>

<h2>Assembling the Galette</h2>
<p>Roll the dough on a lightly floured surface to a 12-inch round, about 1/8-inch thick. Don't stress over the shape—a rough circle is fine. Transfer it to a parchment-lined baking sheet. Now arrange the peach slices in the center, leaving a 2-inch border. I like to fan them out in a single layer, slightly overlapping. Piling them too high makes the center hard to bake through.</p>

<p>Fold the edges of the dough up over the filling, pleating as you go. The pleats are what give the galette its rustic look. Work your way around, pressing gently to seal any cracks. Brush the crust with beaten egg for a shiny, golden finish. Then sprinkle coarse sugar over both the crust and the peaches. That sugar adds a delightful crunch and a bit of sparkle.</p>

<h2>Baking to Perfection</h2>
<p>Bake at 400°F (200°C) for 30-35 minutes. You're looking for a deep golden-brown crust and bubbly, thick peach filling. If the crust browns too quickly, tent the galette loosely with foil after 20 minutes. Every oven runs a bit differently, so check at 30 minutes. The filling should be visibly bubbling in the center—that's the cornstarch doing its job, ensuring the galette slices cleanly instead of being runny.</p>

<h2>Common Mistake to Avoid</h2>
<p>The biggest mistake is not letting the galette cool after baking. I know it's tempting to cut into it right away, but the filling needs time to set. Let it rest on the baking sheet for at least 15 minutes. If you slice too soon, the juices will run out and the crust will get soggy. Patience pays off here.</p>

<h2>Serving and Storing</h2>
<p>Serve this fresh peach galette warm or at room temperature. A scoop of vanilla ice cream or a dollop of lightly sweetened whipped cream complements the fruit beautifully. Leftovers keep covered at room temperature for up to 2 days, but the crust will soften. It's truly best the day it's made. For a gluten-free option, use a 1:1 gluten-free flour blend. For dairy-free, substitute cold coconut oil or vegan butter in the crust. Always use oven mitts when handling hot pans.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>A fresh peach galette is the perfect summer dessert because it's forgiving, adaptable, and shows off the fruit's natural beauty. With a flaky, buttery crust and a simple, not-too-sweet filling, it's a dessert that feels both rustic and special. Make it once, and you'll see why it's a favorite.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Do I have to peel the peaches for a galette?</h3>
<p>No, you don't have to. Leaving the peel on adds color and texture. If you prefer a smoother filling, blanch the peaches briefly to remove the skins easily.</p>
<h3>Can I use frozen peaches?</h3>
<p>Yes, but thaw them completely and pat them very dry with paper towels. Frozen peaches release more liquid, so add an extra teaspoon of cornstarch to the filling to prevent a soggy crust.</p>
<h3>Why is my galette crust tough?</h3>
<p>Tough crust usually means the dough was overworked or too much water was added. Mix just until the dough holds together, and handle it as little as possible. Keeping the butter cold is also crucial for flakiness.</p>
<h3>Can I make the dough ahead of time?</h3>
<p>Absolutely. The dough can be refrigerated for up to 2 days or frozen for up to 3 months. Thaw overnight in the fridge before rolling.</p>
<h3>How do I prevent a soggy bottom crust?</h3>
<p>Bake on a preheated baking sheet or a pizza stone. The direct heat helps the bottom crust crisp up. Also, don't let the filled galette sit too long before baking—get it into the oven right after assembling.</p>
